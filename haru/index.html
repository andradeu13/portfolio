HaruFintechMobile appUX design challenge

A financial coach,
not a bank.

A UX challenge to redesign a personal finance app so people understand where their money goes without doing the work. Haru reads the money for the user and says what it means and what to do next.

TypeUX design challenge
PlatformMobile app
RoleUX / UI design
FocusClarity, motivation, trust

The problem

What we found when talking to users

The challenge started from three findings. They look different but are the same friction: the app asks the user to do the work of understanding their own money.

Finding 1

They can’t see where the money goes

“No entienden en qué se va su dinero.” Numbers exist but meaning doesn’t.

Finding 2

Nobody wants to categorise by hand

“No quieren categorizar gastos a mano.” Manual tagging is a chore that gets abandoned.

Finding 3

Numbers without insight

“Sienten que la app solo tira números, sin insight.” Data is dumped, not interpreted.

The problem isn’t a lack of features. It’s the effort the user is asked to make.

Goal

Understand at a glance

Let the user know their situation in a few seconds.

Goal

Motivate healthy habits

Encourage, without scaring or shaming.

Goal

Look reliable, not generic

Feel like a trustworthy coach, not another banking template.

01 · Empathize

Who it is for, and what she lives with

The persona is a design synthesis of the three findings, a working hypothesis to validate with more interviews. It is not presented as research data.

M

Mariana, 28

Employed, uses a credit card and a few subscriptions, wants to save but doesn’t track.

Goal
Save a bit each month without becoming an accountant.
Context
Checks her phone in short moments: waiting, commuting, after a purchase.
Fear
Finding out too late that she overspent, or being judged by an app.
Habit
Opens banking apps, sees a list of movements, closes them.

Thinks

  • Where did my money go this month?
  • Can I spend this today and still save?
  • I don’t have time to tag every purchase.

Feels

  • Guilt when she sees the totals
  • Overwhelmed by charts without a message
  • Motivated by small wins

Says

  • “No sé en qué gasto tanto.”
  • “¿Cuánto puedo gastar esta semana?”

Does

  • Skips manual tagging
  • Ignores notifications that only repeat numbers
  • Reacts to clear, specific suggestions

02 · Define

Hypothesis and assumptions

The three findings share a root cause, so the answer is to remove work, not add screens.

“If Haru interprets the money for the user, the user no longer has to interpret it themselves.”

Insight without asking

Home surfaces a pattern on its own

An unusual expense, progress toward the goal, a payment due: shown as a message, without the user having to search for it.

Assumptions of the exercise

What is given and what is hypothesis

Given by the brief: read-only access to accounts and cards. Design hypothesis: which patterns are worth showing without being asked.

Understand

No clear picture of the month

A list of transactions doesn’t tell her whether she is doing well.

How might we…answer “how am I doing?” in a few seconds?
Record

Manual categorisation

Tagging each expense is a chore people abandon.

How might we…categorise automatically and ask only when unsure?
Interpret

Numbers without meaning

A chart with no message leaves her to guess the reason.

How might we…explain why spending is high in plain words?
Act

Not knowing what to do

Even with insight, she doesn’t know the next step.

How might we…turn each insight into one concrete action?
Trust

Generic, banking-like feel

Heavy security badges and cold tone make it feel like every other app.

How might we…earn trust through calm, consistency and honesty?

04 · Lateral thinking

Questioning what the brief takes for granted

Before designing screens, I challenged the assumptions behind the brief. These three ideas shaped the product.

01

Connecting accounts is a data mechanism, not a screen.

The user should not spend an onboarding step on it, or see it as the product. It is plumbing.

02

What makes Haru different is how it interprets spending, not how it records it.

Recording is a commodity. Interpretation is the coaching.

03

Payment reminders are the easiest piece to underestimate, and one of the most valuable.

Avoiding interest and late fees is a concrete, immediate benefit that earns trust.

05 · Key screens

3 screens, 3 questions

Each screen is responsible for a single question, so the user never has to decide where to look.

06 · Decisions

From insight to interface

Same structure as my other cases: the pain point, the principle, and how it would be validated. As a design challenge, validation is a proposed plan.

Home

Safe margin as the hero number

Pain point it answers

Needs one number that answers “can I spend?”, not a list.

UX principleVisual hierarchyCognitive load
How to validate

5-second test: can users say whether they are okay?

Home

Alerts as cards with one action each (“Revisar”)

Pain point it answers

Insights that can’t be acted on feel like nagging.

UX principleAffordanceOne primary action
How to validate

Tap-through on alerts; completion of the linked action.

Insights

“Haru procesó tus gastos · 3 requieren validación”

Pain point it answers

Manual categorisation is abandoned; asking only on doubt keeps control.

UX principleAutomation with human in the loopTransparency
How to validate

Share of expenses auto-categorised correctly; validation rate.

Insights

Category capsules and weekday pattern instead of a table

Pain point it answers

Numbers alone don’t explain behaviour.

UX principlePre-attentive processingChunking
How to validate

Comprehension test: “what explains your spending?”.

Insights

A plain-language insight under each visual

Pain point it answers

Charts without a message leave interpretation to the user.

UX principlePlain languageMeaning over data
How to validate

Do users repeat the insight in their own words?

Plan

Available after expenses, tied to the savings goal

Pain point it answers

Spending freedom should be framed against what she wants to save.

UX principleGoal-gradientPositive framing
How to validate

Habit: weekly return rate; goal adherence.

Plan

Concrete, actionable recommendations

Pain point it answers

Insights without a next step don’t change behaviour.

UX principleActionable feedbackReduce decision effort
How to validate

Recommendations acted on per week.

07 · UI

The design communicates calm, not anxiety

Visual choices serve the idea of a coach: friendly, steady and honest.

Mixed radii

Capsules for actions, straighter corners for cards

Avoids the generic “everything rounded” look and signals what is tappable.

Same components

Home, Insights and Plan share cards and type

Consistency builds trust: the user learns the interface once.

Real charts

Donut, bars and capsules by category

Never loose numbers; every figure has a visual form and a sentence.

Trust by absence

No “maximum security” badges or forced seals

Reliability comes from clear language and calm design, not decoration.

  • Tone: calm, not anxious. Alerts say what happened and what to do, without blame.
  • Colour: soft green and yellow for positive and neutral states; warmer tones only for what needs attention.

Next steps

What I would do next

  • Validate the persona with 5–8 interviews and a 5-second test on Home.
  • Test the interpretation: do insights match what users believe explains their spending?
  • Prototype notifications and payment reminders, the piece I think is most undervalued.
  • Define success metrics before building: weekly return rate, recommendations acted on, validation rate.

About this case. This is a design challenge and concept, not a launched product. There are no usage metrics. The persona and journey are design syntheses to be validated.
